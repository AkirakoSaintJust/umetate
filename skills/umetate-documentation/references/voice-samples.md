# Voice samples

These are short excerpts from the author's own writing: reviews, forum posts, and
notes. They are here for rhythm rather than content, so read them before a
front-door page, a manual intro, or a guide or study, then read `voice.md` for the
rules.

The full corpus is not checked in, because it is the author's personal film and
music writing rather than part of the framework. The excerpts are enough to hear the
rhythm.

The published manual is a restrained version of this, with no film opinions and no
swearing. The hand rewrite keeps the digressions, the slang, and the jokes, and the
sentence is the same.

## Guide rewrite

These are from the author's rewrite of the first 70 lines of the K5/K5000 guide,
and they are the target for a guide or study rather than a manual page.

### Plain category opening

> The K5 and K5000 are two additive synths from Kawai. Additive synths are rare, so
> they're an interesting case study.
>
> They came out at different times and the K5's shortcomings probably were part of
> what motivated the K5000's design.

The model draft opened with a balanced thesis, and the author cut it for a plain
category, a reason to care, and a causal guess. "Probably" is the right hedge. The
plain version is the target.

### Define the term

> A periodic musical sound is a stack of sine waves at multiples of a fundamental.
> Each of these sines is called a harmonic, and the fundamental determines the
> sound's "pitch".

The guide can explain a term the audience may not know, so don't assume the reader
already knows what a harmonic is.

### Long explanatory sentence

> The upper harmonics can be strong at the attack and vanish in the sustain, and
> then the fundamental can fade in late whilst the even harmonics over from the odd
> ones, or a group of high partials can pulse while the fundamental stays still,
> and so on.

The author wrote one long sentence where the model wrote four short ones. Keep the
long sentence. Fix "over from" to "take over from". Keep "whilst" and "and so on".

### Connectivity join

> Each additive source holds 64 harmonics, with two possible settings: Low is 1 to
> 64, and High is 65 to 128.

The model draft wrote that as three sentences, and the author joined the
specification to its main clause with "with" and a colon. The same move runs through
the guide:

| Draft | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| Harmonic 64 is missing. It is a documented quirk. | Harmonic 64 is missing, which is a documented quirk of the instrument. |
| Use Full Mode when you have a specific reason. | Use Full Mode only when you have a specific reason. |
| The groups are all harmonics, odd, even, octave-related and fifth-related. | The groups are: all harmonics, odd, even, octave-related and fifth-related. |
| Sources are at the end. | (see sources at the end) |

Attach a fact or an explanation with a relative clause. Attach a condition to its
action with "only when". Use a colon for the list that follows. A guide that states every
fact as its own sentence has lost the rhythm, even when every sentence is clear.

### Parenthetical surprise

> ... whilst the K5000 has got one envelope per harmonic (!)

"Has got" and the parenthetical "(!)" are voice rather than P41 or P14.

### Factual contrast

> A K5 Single patch has two sources, containing a complete voice path, rather than
> just the oscillator.

"Rather than just" is a factual contrast that names both sides, so don't rewrite it
into a positive-only sentence.

### Scope and audience

> This guide is for players and sound designers. ... it isn't a technical manual,
> so the diagrams are purely explanatory rather than factory schematics.

The scope and the audience are stated early. The slash in "anecdotal/historical
records" is a category label, not prose punctuation.

### Replacing the negation

The author removed nearly every "It's a X, not a Y" and "It's not an X" setup, and
the replacements are the lesson.

| Draft | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| They are not the same idea at two sizes. | Cut. They came out at different times and the K5's shortcomings probably motivated the K5000. |
| The K5000 isn't a K5 with a bigger harmonic count. It's a different architecture. | Cut. The causal link replaces the contrast. |
| The interesting part is not the list. It is... | The interesting part for sound design is... |
| They aren't arbitrary-partial resynthesizers. | You can't add inharmonic partials... |
| The useful question is not how many harmonics... It is... | The K5 provides four envelopes, whilst the K5000 has one per harmonic. |
| a complete voice path, not a single oscillator | a complete voice path, rather than just the oscillator |
| a documented quirk, not a mistake in your patch | for some quirky reason (but keep "documented") |
| a specialised resource, not an automatic upgrade | more of a specialised tool than an automatic upgrade |

The author did not remove factual limits ("you can't add...", "it isn't a
universal system") or a scope contrast ("purely explanatory rather than factory
schematics"). The target is the needless label negation, not every "not".

The test: a negation earns its place only when it carries a causal link, a factual
comparison, an explanation of something potentially counterintuitive, a real
limit, or a correction of a misconception. If it carries none of those, cut it.

### Conversational correction

> But that doesn't mean one is necessarily better than the other! They've just got
> different personalities.
>
> That is just a tour-rig claim, though, with no album credits to back it up.
>
> They do sound similar, but there's no recorded session credit to verify it.

The author keeps the negative when it corrects a real inference or names missing
evidence. "Just", "though", and "more of a" scale the claim without turning it into
a label contrast. The third example replaces the label with the evidence: the
sounds are similar, but there is no session credit. The earlier "That is a
difference in personality, not a ranking" was correct but stiffer, and the spoken
correction is the voice.

### Anecdotal aside

> Anecdotally, though, *Revolutions* is known to be made out of D50 presets mostly,
> so I'd really like to give it a closer listen and track the possible K5 sounds.

"Anecdotally, though" marks the lower confidence, whilst "I'd really like to" is a
genuine intention rather than a marketing hook. One aside per section at most.

### Cut the summary verdict

> When ideas did not come, he would stop watching the screen and experiment until a
> new sound appeared.

The author cut the old ending, "That is the K5 in its natural habitat: a
professional tool under deadline." A concrete detail can land without a closing
verdict that restates it.

### Causal recommendation

> Its restrictions are instructive, so it is the synth to learn additive synthesis
> on.

The old version had two short verdicts: "It is the instrument to learn on. Its
restrictions are the lesson." The author joined the cause and the consequence with
"so", so the recommendation explains itself.

### Aphorism

| Draft | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| Its restrictions are the lesson. | The K5's two sources and four envelopes are enough to learn how harmonic groups move. |
| Two sources with clear jobs beat six sources with vague ones. | Start with two sources and add another only when you can name its job. |
| They are instruments for composing the inside of a sound. | Both reward a player who wants to build a timbre from its partials and shape how they move. |
| The filter is useful, and it is also a trap. | A filter sweep can make a weak harmonic construction sound exciting for a moment and hide what the K5 is doing. |
| Hybridisation is musically intelligent. | Additive and PCM cover different jobs. |

An aphorism is a short, universal-sounding verdict that could go on a poster, and
the quasi-aphorism is the same move with a concrete noun, such as "its restrictions
are the lesson". Replace both with the concrete reason or the instruction. A
specific verdict is fine. A slogan is not.

### Comma-not adversative

| Draft | Rewrite |
| --- | --- |
| single precision, not `double` | single precision only, so keep `double` out of the DSP |
| whole waveforms, not partials | whole waveforms rather than individual partials |
| the framework, not the glue | part of the framework rather than the app glue |
| recomputed at block rate, not per sample | recomputed at block rate rather than per sample |

The comma-not form reads like a spec sheet. Use "rather than", a separate sentence,
or a "but" clause. Keep it only when the second half is a precise factual limit and
the sentence would be clumsy without it.

### One loss

> Harmonic 64 is missing for some quirky reason.

The casual phrase is fine, but it drops "documented". Keep the fact: "Harmonic 64
is missing for some quirky reason. It's a documented quirk, not a mistake in your
patch."

### Numbers

The first 70 lines moved from 689 words in 58 sentences (mean 11.6, median 9.5,
standard deviation 8.5, longest 52) to 737 words in 41 sentences (mean 17.6,
median 14, standard deviation 16.7, longest 80). The first-person count stayed at
three. The change is sentence structure and explanatory connectives, not
self-reference.

The first 170 lines are 2,100 words in 147 sentences: a 13.8-word mean, a 12-word
median, an 11.0-word standard deviation. The opening is more connected than the rest
of the first draft. The connectivity pass is how you close that distance.

## Panel section

A panel section teaches movement before it lists controls. The Type P.fm1 manual
opens the panel part with the surface, then the navigation rule, then a table per
context.

> Press a context button to open that context at its first page. Press the same
> button again to move to the next page. `SELECT` also pages forward and back. The
> pages wrap at the ends.
>
> `HOME` pops back to the home context. Press `HOME` again to step through the home
> pages.

Then every page gets a row with its four knob assignments and its display:

| Page | KNOB 1 | KNOB 2 | KNOB 3 | KNOB 4 | Screen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| OSCILLATOR | RATIO | LEVEL | FEEDBACK | WAVE | A scope of the selected operator. |

The tables are plain, and the voice sits in the navigation prose and the short page
notes. Don't write a control list and call it a panel section.

## Hand rewrite

These are from the author's hand rewrite of the Type P.fm1 manual, and they are the
target for manual voice. The first release was flatter. Read these before a manual
page.

### Design note

> I'm a hardcore fan of phase modulation synths, so for the longest time I've kept
> thinking about things I wanted to change on the Yamahas (and Yamaha emulators)
> I've been using over the years. That's where Type P.fm1 comes from.

First person, a specific history, a long setup: the next paragraph can start with
"Originally" and carry the constraint. The preface is front-door register rather than
a spec sheet.

### The list is content

> 4. envelope graphs are essential, especially if you've got rate-level envelopes,
> because they help massively with the sheer amount of params, and provide an easy
> overview of what the patch is like at a given moment.

The list is the author's seven design observations, and it is content rather than
rule-of-three padding. The sentence is long and comma-heavy but reads as one
thought. A manual can carry it.

### Exclamation cluster

> 6. velocity and key scaling are cool but they've got to live in their little
> corner because there are just so! many! params!

The three exclamation marks are deliberate, because they are in the design note
rather than a procedure. Keep the cluster. Don't spray it through the page.

### Factual contrast

> It's not an emulation of any one Yamaha chip in particular, and the DSP was
> implemented from scratch, but it **is** a DX-style synth, and it **does** take
> cues from the way Yamaha emulators are implemented, particularly ymfm and Dexed.

Both sides are real, because the bold `is` and `does` are deliberate contrast
rather than random inline bold. This is not the "not just X, it's Y" hook.

### Concrete sound, then the aside

> Whole-number ratios sound clear, odd ratios sound metallic/reedy, and non-integer
> ratios sound clangy and inharmonic, which can be industrial noise or struck-type
> sounds like marimbas, vibraphones, weird glassy pads, and the Taco Bell bell (I've
> never been to a Taco Bell but being terminally online has taught me Americans find
> this really funny.)

The aside names the sound and is one sentence. Keep it. The sentence is a run-on. A
manual edit can split it after "inharmonic" without losing the voice. Do not cut the
Taco Bell aside to make the page neutral.

### Mechanism

> All operators have feedback, but traditionally it shows up the most on carriers
> and on the top operator of a stack. That's because of the way Yamaha chips work:
> the operators were always computed in the same order and the one with feedback
> was always the first one.

"That's because" is the teaching voice rather than filler, because the history
explains why the panel behaves the way it does. Keep it.

### Design aside

> Personally I find it sounds nice when a patch swells up, goes past the threshold
> for a bit, then comes back under again (that's why I added it).

One aside, one design reason. It earns its place. This is the model for a personal
note in a parameter section.

### Self-description

> I'm more of a sound design gal, I fully sequence my music and I prefer trackers to
> little boxes for sequences/composition/arrangement, but it should work for those
> users as well.

Keep "gal". Do not neutralise it. The sentence tells the reader who the author is,
how they work, and who the synth is for.

### End on an instruction

> Try looking at the operator oscilloscopes to check out how the cascading phase
> modulation progressively warps each waveform!

The earlier draft ended on "the whole of FM in one knob". The author replaced the
aphorism with a concrete next action. Do that.

## Anti-slop front door

These are from the hand-written README, and they set the register for a release front
door and for the documentation skill's own promise.

> I've tried to calibrate the resulting prose from my own personal writing, which
> might sound a bit chummy, nerdy and British. If that's not your thing I appreciate
> your position, but I'm going to feel guilty and embarrassed if you use umetate to
> make something and publish it with awful slop marketing blurb docs. Please take
> care of the community.

The aside admits a taste, names the stake, and asks for care. The embarrassment is
real, so the request lands. A line like "we are passionate about empowering
creators" has the same topic and no person in it.

> A hand-written `publish-readme.md` in the project is copied verbatim as the release
> front door. Without one, `publish` writes a README from its template. Ideally,
> hand-write your own if possible. Show that amount of passion for your own project
> as well, I beg you, or at least use my interview functionality (see next) so that
> your LLM agent has an idea of why you made your synth and what you want to tell
> your users.

The instruction is direct, the aside is in brackets, and the ask is specific, which
is the front-door voice. Don't tidy it into "authors are encouraged to provide a
bespoke release README".

> We're making open source synths for fun and musical discovery, so it'd be great if
> we were at least kind enough to each other to actually document how to use our
> synths and tell the community about the ideas, hopes and passions we wanted to
> share with fellow m-vavers, right?

"Right?" is a real question tag rather than a marketing hook, so keep it when the
author wrote it. Don't replace it with "join us on this journey".

## Long setup, short landing

> The shorts are tied by themes of music or sound, but they engage with it to
> varying degrees and with one of the shorts I don't even see the connection at
> all, other than having been directed by a well-known musician.

The next paragraph begins "The interludes featuring Gwar are mostly terrible." The
long sentence sets up the verdict. Copy the patience, not the length. A manual can
carry a 40-word sentence if the clauses belong together, then land it in five.

## Verdict first

> The interludes featuring Gwar are mostly terrible. They've got some funny,
> campy, practical effects at times, but the script is painful.

The verdict is the first sentence, and the second gives the exception without
softening it. This is how to be fair without hedging. "Mostly terrible" is a real
judgment rather than a vibe.

## Concede, then return

> It's well made as a photography and editing exercice, but not too interesting.

One concession, one verdict, with no "however" stack and no "on the other hand"
chain. The spelling mistake is left in the corpus and corrected in published docs.
The shape is what matters: credit the craft, then say what it does for you.

## Answer the question

> The CPU is fine (two cores that go up to 320MHz), but I don't think there's
> enough memory for a real-time Python interpreter. The good thing is the processor
> can read/execute stuff directly from flash (XIP)!

This is the shape of a manual answer. Direct answer, reason, caveat, good news. The
number is in the bracket. The caveat is plain. The exclamation is genuine, not
decoration.

## Compare with something real

> Negative feedback is, roughly, a lot like an operator (or more, or a stack) at
> ratio 2 into an operator at ratio 1.

"Roughly" signals the approximation, and the comparison uses the reader's own
vocabulary before the precise version follows. A comparison to "warmth" or
"character" would tell the reader nothing.

## Negate a fact, not a strawman

> It's not exactly the same sound but that's it basically.

This is how the corpus negates: it does not say "it's not just a sound, it's a
texture", but states the limit and stops. If the difference matters, name both sides.
If it does not, don't build a sentence around it.

## Name the structure

> Family, state and class are roasted relentlessly.

A whole analysis in one line. The nouns are the structure, not the mood. Use this
when explaining a design choice: name the constraint, the incentive, or the part
that does the work. Do not name the vibe.

## Bottom line

> Bottom line: the myth that GEMS sounds bad annoys me to no end.

A plain summary marker, then a personal stake. Use it once in a long page. Not every
page, and never as a substitute for the argument.

## Correct yourself

> I've done a bit of audio DSP work with C# and sometimes you get some very dumb
> asm, although I'm guessing the JIT or a good PGO profile would help.

The claim, then the correction, with the hedge on the cause rather than the
experience. The reader trusts the experience because the author does not overclaim
the explanation.

## Recommend it

> Check out the "Live '77" version of The Last One for an entry point if you want.

An imperative, a specific record, a condition. No "we recommend", no "you might want
to consider". The reader can take it or leave it.

## One aside

> I'm a bit anxious about showing what I'm doing to anyone because, besides being
> AI-generated code, the scope is now far larger.

One aside, plain, no self-pity. It earns its place because it explains the state of
the project. A manual can carry one of these in the preface. Not one per section.

## Use the reader's world

> kind of like Angular if you know about web dev?

The analogy is specific to the reader's world, and the question tag checks whether
the reader is the right reader. In docs, drop the tag if the audience is already
clear. Keep the specificity.

## One line is a paragraph

> Exposition, exposition. Exposition.

The repetition is the joke. A one-line paragraph is a beat. Use it once or twice per
page for a verdict or a limit. Never in the middle of a procedure.

## Understate it

> Not much to say about this one.

A blunt opener that lowers the stakes. The reader knows the review will be short.
The docs can use the same move before a short section: "Not much to say about this
one. The filter has one control."

## Name the machine

> People had Atari STs with patch editor software that allowed for loading patch
> banks into your synth. Atari STs in particular because they came with MIDI ports
> and they ran Cubase, so they were ideal for production.

The specific machine, the specific ports, the specific software. This is how to be
concrete. "Old computers" would be true and useless.

## Ask a real question

> Is The Mystery Man similar to a vampire? He has to be invited in and lurks in
> your house, shooting tapes of you being violent in moments you don't remember,
> alternatively threatening you and helping you kill people.

A genuine question, then the evidence, rather than a hook. Use a real question when
you are actually going to answer it. Don't use a question to announce a section.
